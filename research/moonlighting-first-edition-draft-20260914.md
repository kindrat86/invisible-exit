# Invisible Exit moonlighting and side-business clause reference

Status: internal publishing draft only. Do not publish.

Intended canonical URL: `/research/moonlighting-and-side-business-clauses`

This reference is for an employed founder asking a narrow question: what should I check before I build or operate a paid side business while keeping my job? It is not legal advice. The answer depends on the contract, the work actually performed, the employer's business, the information and equipment used, and the state law that applies.

The federal baseline is deliberately modest. The Federal Trade Commission's Non-Compete Rule was set aside by a federal district court and was later removed from the Code of Federal Regulations. This draft therefore does not promise a nationwide federal permission to moonlight. It starts with state law and preserves contract, confidentiality, trade-secret, conflict, and loyalty questions. [claim:federal-overlay]

## How to use this reference

Read the row for the state whose law actually governs the employment relationship. Then check the agreement for provisions covering outside employment, competition, customer contact, confidentiality, intellectual property, inventions, and use of company property. A rule about post-employment competition may not answer whether active competition is allowed during employment. A void noncompete also does not make theft of trade secrets or use of employer resources lawful.

Each row below is a compact issue-spotter, not a verdict. Every legal proposition points to a claim in the companion machine-readable source manifest. Dollar thresholds are intentionally omitted because several change over time. The release version must be re-verified against the current source text on its publication date.

## California

California broadly voids employment noncompetes unless a statutory exception applies. The current statute directs broad reading of that rule, and a separate provision reaches contracts that are void under the chapter even when signed elsewhere. [claim:ca-noncompete]

For a side project, invention ownership is a separate check. California excludes an invention developed entirely on the employee's own time without employer equipment, facilities, supplies, or trade secrets, but preserves exceptions when the invention relates to the employer's business or anticipated research, or results from work performed for the employer. [claim:ca-inventions]

**Draft answer:** a narrow employment noncompete is not automatically saved merely because it is narrow. Keep the project genuinely separate, then review the invention-assignment exceptions and any confidentiality or loyalty duties before treating the answer as permission to compete.

Last source verification: September 2026. [claim:ca-verified]

## Washington

Washington directly protects certain lower-paid employees who take another job, contract independently, or become self-employed. The protection is tied to a multiple of the applicable state minimum wage, so this draft omits a dollar amount. Safety, normal scheduling expectations, loyalty, and conflicts remain exceptions. [claim:wa-additional-work]

Washington separately defines noncompetition covenants broadly while excluding confidentiality, trade-secret, and nonsolicitation agreements from that definition. Its current code also contains a future version under which noncompetition covenants become void. That future rule must not be described as current law before its effective date. [claim:wa-noncompete]

**Draft answer:** lower-paid employees have an express statutory starting point for nonconflicting additional work, but it is not a license to create safety problems, ignore normal scheduling, misuse information, or compete disloyally.

Last source verification: September 2026. [claim:wa-verified]

## New York

New York's lawful-activities statute protects listed categories, but its definition of recreational activity requires that the employee receive no compensation. A paid side business should not be presented as protected recreation under that section. [claim:ny-statute]

New York's official law reporter also records that an employee may not compete with the employer's business during employment. The cited case involved opening a directly competing business and supports a clear distinction between harmless outside activity and active in-employment competition. [claim:ny-loyalty]

**Draft answer:** do not rely on the lawful-recreation rule for paid moonlighting. Review the contract and whether the venture directly competes with the employer while the employment relationship continues.

Last source verification: September 2026. [claim:ny-verified]

## Texas

Texas permits a covenant not to compete only when it is part of an otherwise enforceable agreement and its limits on time, geography, and activity are reasonable and no broader than needed to protect a business interest. The current official text includes amendments that older mirrors omit. [claim:tx-covenant]

A published federal appellate opinion applying Texas law says an at-will employee may plan and take preparatory steps to compete, but may not appropriate trade secrets, solicit employer customers while still employed, take customer lists, use employer funds or staff for the future venture, or act to harm the employer. [claim:tx-preparation]

**Draft answer:** preparation can be lawful while disloyal execution is not. Separate planning from customer solicitation, confidential information, company resources, and active harm.

Last source verification: September 2026. [claim:tx-verified]

## Illinois

The Illinois Freedom to Work Act regulates post-employment covenants. It uses an earnings gate and also requires adequate consideration, a valid employment relationship, protection of a legitimate business interest, no undue hardship, and no injury to the public. This draft intentionally omits the changing earnings amounts and review-period length. [claim:il-covenant]

**Draft answer:** the statute may defeat or limit a post-employment noncompete, but it does not create general permission to run a competing business during employment. Check the outside-work clause and loyalty, confidentiality, and conflict rules separately.

Last source verification: September 2026. [claim:il-verified]

## Colorado

Colorado's current framework generally voids covenants that restrict a person's right to receive compensation for labor, subject to enumerated exceptions. A trade-secret-protection exception can apply to a highly compensated worker when the covenant is no broader than reasonably necessary. The compensation threshold changes, so no dollar figure appears here. [claim:co-covenant]

**Draft answer:** begin with the statutory presumption against compensation-restricting covenants, then test every claimed exception. Do not confuse that analysis with permission to use employer trade secrets or compete during working time.

Last source verification: September 2026. [claim:co-verified]

## Massachusetts

Massachusetts defines the employment noncompetition agreement in its cited statute as a restriction on competitive activity after employment ends. The statute excludes several groups and distinguishes noncompetition agreements from confidentiality and nonsolicitation agreements. [claim:ma-statute]

The state's highest court has also held that trusted executive employees owe loyalty and may not actively compete with the employer during employment even without an express covenant. The decision is specific to trusted executives and should not be generalized to every employee without further authority. [claim:ma-loyalty]

**Draft answer:** a post-employment covenant analysis is only half the question. Trusted executives face a distinct in-employment loyalty rule, and confidentiality or solicitation restrictions may survive even where a noncompete does not.

Last source verification: September 2026. [claim:ma-verified]

## North Dakota

North Dakota's employment-discrimination statute protects lawful activity away from the employer's premises during nonworking hours when that activity does not directly conflict with essential business-related interests. A bona fide occupational qualification tied rationally to the work remains an exception. [claim:nd-statute]

State decisions interpret the no-direct-conflict requirement as a meaningful limit rather than a blanket shield. The source inventory records a side-business dispute in which conflicting off-hours business activity was not protected under the version then applied. [claim:nd-cases]

**Draft answer:** nonconflicting lawful off-duty work has an express statutory anchor. A side business that directly conflicts with an essential employer interest may fall outside it, so the facts matter.

Last source verification: September 2026. [claim:nd-verified]

## Virginia

Virginia's official code defines a covenant not to compete as a restraint that applies after employment ends, and its low-wage framework preserves separate confidentiality and trade-secret rules. The official session-law source also changed the section in 2026. [claim:va-framework]

## Practical checklist before acting

- Identify which state's law and forum provisions the agreement names.
- Separate an outside-employment ban from a post-employment noncompete.
- Search for customer and employee solicitation restrictions.
- Map every invention or intellectual-property assignment clause to the work you will actually do.
- Keep side-business devices, accounts, source code, working time, files, and expenses separate from the employer.
- Do not use trade secrets, customer lists, internal pricing, roadmaps, or other confidential information.
- Record what you built, when you built it, and which resources you used.
- Get qualified legal advice before launching when the venture competes, touches the employer's customers, or depends on disputed intellectual property.
